Chapter 02 — The work

Description of the work

Overview

The Winged Victory of Samothrace is a monument of Greek sculpture from the Hellenistic period, found on the island of Samothrace. It shows the goddess Nike (Victory) at the end of her flight, alighting on the prow of a warship.

The wings

The great wings, swept back behind her, give the figure all of its momentum. Only the left wing is ancient: the right one is a plaster restoration, made as a mirror image of the original.

The chiton

The head and arms are missing. Victory wears a long tunic (chiton) of very fine fabric. The sculptor multiplied the effects of drapery, with areas where the cloth clings to the body and reveals its forms.

The drapery

The wind seems to press the fabric flat, especially across the belly, while elsewhere it gathers into deep, shadowed folds, as between the legs, where the cloak billows out behind the goddess.

Chapter 03 — The discoverer

Charles Champoiseau

Charles-François-Noël Champoiseau (1 May 1830, Tours – 29 June 1909, Paris) was a French diplomat and archaeologist, credited with the discovery of the Winged Victory of Samothrace in 1863. While posted to Adrianople, he decided to excavate on Samothrace in 1862, and found the Winged Victory there the following year.

He sent it to the Louvre to enrich the museum's collection, which he added to again in 1867 with two torsos discovered at Actium. He is among the first donors and missionaries named on the commemorative plaque honouring those who did most to enrich the Louvre.

Chapter 04 — History

Timeline

190BC
  1. c. 190 BC

    An offering to the Great Gods

    Carved in the Hellenistic period, the statue is set up in the Sanctuary of the Great Gods on Samothrace, probably to commemorate a naval victory.

  2. 1830

    Born in Tours

    Charles Champoiseau, the future diplomat and archaeologist, is born in Tours.

  3. 15 April 1863

    The discovery

    While posted to Adrianople, Champoiseau unearths the body of the Victory on Samothrace, broken into many fragments.

  4. 1864

    Arrival in Paris

    The fragments of the statue join the collections of the Louvre.

  5. 1879

    The ship

    Champoiseau returns to Samothrace and has the grey marble blocks of the ship's prow sent to Paris.

  6. 1884

    The Daru staircase

    Reunited with its base, the Victory is installed at the top of the Daru staircase, where it still commands the view.

  7. 1950

    The right hand

    Excavations on Samothrace uncover the statue's right hand, now displayed in a nearby case.

  8. 2014

    Restored

    After almost a year of restoration, the Victory returns, gleaming, to the top of the Daru staircase.
